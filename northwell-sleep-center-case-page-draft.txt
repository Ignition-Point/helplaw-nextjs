What You Need to Know

When patients arrived at a medical facility for a sleep study or a physical therapy appointment, they had a right to expect that the most private moments of their visit would remain private. For nearly two years, that expectation was violated.

Between August 2022 and April 2024, a sleep technician named Sanjai Syamaprasad secretly recorded patients, visitors, and staff in the bathrooms of two Northwell Health facilities in Great Neck, New York — the Northwell Health Sleep Disorders Center and STARS Rehabilitation. He used hidden cameras disguised as smoke detectors, attaching them to bathroom walls before his shifts and removing them afterward to download the footage.

Whether you visited one of these facilities for treatment or worked there as an employee, or whether you're here for someone in your family who may have been affected, reaching out does not start a lawsuit or require you to commit to anything. How Were Patients and Staff Secretly Recorded? (Dark)

Syamaprasad purchased a covert recording device designed to look like a standard smoke detector in August 2022. Over the following two years, he developed a routine: he attached the device to bathroom walls using Velcro discs before his shifts at the Sleep Disorders Center and STARS Rehabilitation, both located in the same building complex at 145–155 Community Drive in Great Neck, New York.

The cameras were placed in areas where patients, staff, and visitors had the highest expectation of privacy — restrooms and changing areas. In some cases, devices were positioned directly above toilets.

Syamaprasad downloaded the footage onto an SD card after each shift. Prosecutors later recovered over 300 videos from his devices. He also viewed footage on his work-issued laptop while on duty at the medical facility, which is how a coworker discovered the recordings in April 2024.

The recordings captured a wide range of individuals: adult patients undergoing sleep studies, physical therapy patients at STARS Rehabilitation, medical staff, visitors, and minors. Law enforcement confirmed that at least one child was identifiable in the recovered footage, and civil lawsuits allege that numerous minors were recorded.

Many of these individuals may not yet know they were recorded. The notification letters were not sent until May 2025, more than a year after Northwell Health discovered the cameras and reported the incident to law enforcement.

How Did This Go Undetected for Nearly Two Years? (Dark)

The hidden cameras operated across two facilities in the same building complex for approximately twenty months before discovery. Civil lawsuits allege that Northwell Health's oversight failures contributed to the duration of the surveillance.

Facility Security Gaps

Hospitals and medical facilities have a heightened responsibility to secure private areas — restrooms, changing rooms, and examination spaces. The fact that an employee was able to repeatedly install and remove fake smoke detectors in multiple bathrooms without detection suggests a lack of routine facility inspections in sensitive patient areas. The cameras were disguised as standard smoke detectors, but they were attached with Velcro and moved between shifts. Standard security protocols should have flagged unfamiliar or unauthorized devices in patient areas.

Discovery by a Coworker, Not by the Institution

The recordings were discovered in April 2024 only because a coworker noticed Syamaprasad watching footage of someone using a bathroom on his work-issued laptop. Northwell Health's internal systems did not detect the cameras. The institution became aware of the surveillance because of individual observation, not institutional oversight.

The One-Year Notification Delay

After discovering the cameras in April 2024, Northwell Health reported the matter to the Nassau County District Attorney's Office. However, the hospital did not send notification letters to potentially affected patients and staff until May 2025 — more than a full year later. Northwell has stated that it delayed notification at the instruction of law enforcement to avoid interfering with the criminal investigation. Civil attorneys have argued that no law prevented the hospital from notifying patients that their privacy had been compromised. During that year, patients and employees continued to visit the facility without knowing what had occurred there.

Who Is Responsible?

Sanjai Syamaprasad

Syamaprasad, a 48-year-old sleep technician from Brooklyn, installed the cameras, recorded the footage, and attempted to destroy the evidence when he was discovered. He was indicted on five counts of Unlawful Surveillance in the Second Degree and two counts of Tampering with Physical Evidence. He pleaded guilty in July 2025. Despite prosecutors requesting 7 to 21 years in prison, Judge Meryl Berkowitz sentenced him to six months in jail and five years of probation. He is required to register as a sex offender under the New York State Sex Offender Registration Act (SORA).

Syamaprasad was also a part-time employee at the Weill Cornell Center for Sleep Medicine in Manhattan. No recordings have been confirmed at that location, but the Manhattan District Attorney's Office investigated his conduct there, and Weill Cornell barred him from the premises in April 2024.

Northwell Health

Northwell Health is New York State's largest healthcare provider and private employer. As the operator of the facilities where the cameras were placed, Northwell is the primary defendant in the civil lawsuits. Claims against Northwell allege gross negligence, failure to supervise employees, failure to inspect facilities for unauthorized surveillance devices, and failure to timely notify affected patients and staff.

The civil lawsuits argue that Northwell had a duty to maintain secure environments in areas where patients undress or use restroom facilities. The fact that hidden cameras operated undetected for nearly two years across two facilities in the same building complex forms the basis of the negligence claims. The year-long delay in notifying potential victims is cited as a separate failure — one that compounded the harm by denying affected individuals the opportunity to seek legal and emotional support sooner.

What Are Your Legal Options? (Dark)

Can a civil lawsuit be filed even though Syamaprasad was only sentenced to six months in jail?

Yes. A civil lawsuit is separate from the criminal case. The standard of proof in a civil case is different from a criminal case, and civil claims focus on compensation and accountability — not criminal punishment. Over 400 individuals have already filed civil lawsuits against Northwell Health.

Who can file a civil claim?

Anyone who visited the Northwell Health Sleep Disorders Center or STARS Rehabilitation between August 2022 and April 2024 and may have used the restroom facilities during their visit may have a potential claim. This includes patients, employees, visitors, and family members of minors who were recorded.

Can Northwell Health be held responsible for what an employee did?

Civil lawsuits in this case target Northwell Health as the institution, not just the individual employee. The legal theory of negligent supervision holds that an employer can be liable when it fails to exercise reasonable care in overseeing its employees and maintaining safe facilities. The claims allege that Northwell's failure to detect the cameras over a nearly two-year period, and its year-long delay in notifying victims, constitute gross negligence.

What laws apply to this case?

Syamaprasad was convicted under New York Penal Law § 250.45, Unlawful Surveillance in the Second Degree, which makes it a felony to install a recording device to surreptitiously view a person in a place where they have a reasonable expectation of privacy. The civil lawsuits are grounded in claims of negligence, negligent supervision, and infliction of emotional distress. Medical facilities are also bound by patient confidentiality laws, including HIPAA. Additionally, New York Civil Rights Law § 52-b provides a private right of action for the unlawful recording or dissemination of intimate images.

What about the statute of limitations?

For general personal injury and negligence claims in New York, the statute of limitations is typically three years. Privacy-specific intentional torts may have a shorter one-year window under CPLR 215(3). Because the recordings occurred between 2022 and 2024 and many victims only learned of the breach in 2025, timely legal action is important. Consulting with an attorney can help clarify which deadlines apply to your specific situation.

What if I didn't receive a notification letter from Northwell?

Not every individual who visited these facilities during the relevant period may have been included in the initial notification. If you visited either the Sleep Disorders Center or STARS Rehabilitation between August 2022 and April 2024, you may still have a potential claim regardless of whether you received a letter.

Several developments have changed the legal landscape for individuals affected by the Northwell Health hidden camera surveillance.

The criminal case has concluded. Syamaprasad pleaded guilty in July 2025 and was sentenced in November 2025. While many victims and advocates have criticized the sentence as too lenient — prosecutors had requested 7 to 21 years — the guilty plea establishes the underlying facts of the case.

The notification letters sent in May 2025 alerted thousands of individuals to the possibility that they were recorded. For many, the letter was the first indication that their privacy had been violated.

Over 400 individuals have already filed civil lawsuits against Northwell Health. Multiple law firms are actively investigating additional claims. Similar hidden camera cases in medical settings have resulted in substantial settlements — Johns Hopkins Hospital paid $190 million in 2014 to settle claims related to the actions of Dr. Nikita Levy, who secretly recorded patients over a period of years.

The statute of limitations for some claims may be as short as one year, making it important for anyone who believes they may have been affected to consult with an attorney while legal options remain available.
